Inside the creative process: from messy notes to a clear interface

A look at the unglamorous middle of design work: collecting too much, finding the problem, and editing until the interface has a point of view.

Collect before you decide

The beginning of a project is usually messy. I collect screenshots, articles, interview notes, competitor observations, half-formed ideas, and questions that may not have answers yet. The point is not to find a reference to copy. It is to build enough context that the problem starts to show its shape.

I keep this phase deliberately loose. A folder of images, a running document, and a few pages of notes are enough. The temptation to make a beautiful moodboard too early is strong, but premature polish can lock in an idea before the problem is understood.

Make the problem visible

At some point the notes need to become a diagram. I map the people involved, the information they need, the decisions they make, and the places where the current experience breaks down. The diagram is not a deliverable. It is a thinking tool.

This is where a vague request like 'make the dashboard better' turns into a specific question: which decision is the dashboard supposed to support, and what information is missing when someone tries to make it? A clear problem is easier to design for and much easier to explain.

Sketch in constraints

I like to sketch with constraints already in place: a small screen, a short attention span, a limited content model, or a single primary action. Constraints make sketches more useful because they force a point of view.

Most of these sketches are bad. That is the job. A bad sketch is cheap, and it makes the good idea easier to recognise. The goal is to explore enough directions that the final interface is a decision rather than the first thing that looked acceptable.

Build the smallest convincing thing

Once a direction feels promising, I build the smallest version that can be tested: a key flow, a real piece of content, a responsive layout, and enough states to see how it behaves. A working fragment teaches more than a complete mockup of a product that does not exist yet.

This is also where writing becomes part of the design. Labels, empty states, and error messages often reveal gaps in the interaction model that the visuals were hiding.

Edit ruthlessly

The final pass is mostly subtraction. Remove the second accent colour. Remove the animation that does not explain anything. Remove the feature that exists because it was easy to draw. What remains should have a reason to be there.

A clear interface is not the result of a single inspired moment. It is the result of a lot of small decisions, most of which are invisible in the final screenshot. The process is messy, but the outcome does not have to be.

Key takeaways

  • Collect context before committing to a visual direction.
  • Turn vague requests into specific design questions.
  • Sketch inside constraints to find a point of view.
  • Edit the interface until every element has a reason to exist.
