The brief

Most homeware stores ask shoppers to process dozens of products at once. Aurora imagines the opposite: a storefront that unfolds slowly, with room for material stories, maker notes, and the practical details people actually need before buying.

The concept needed to feel warm without becoming precious. It also had to work as a real storefront: searchable products, clear pricing, saved carts, and a checkout flow that does not lose the calm tone along the way.

Starting with content

I began with a content model rather than a layout. Products, collections, maker profiles, and care guides were mapped as reusable blocks so the store could publish a new story without redesigning a page. That model shaped the navigation, the collection template, and the rhythm of the product detail page.

The visual system uses a warm paper background, a deep ink text colour, and a single indigo accent. Product photography does the loudest work. Type is deliberately quiet, with generous line height and a compact, readable product specification table.

Designing the flow

The catalogue can be filtered by room, material, and availability. Filters update in place, preserve keyboard focus, and announce the result count to assistive technology. Product cards expose the essential information without a hover-only interaction.

The cart is a side drawer with a visible close control, focus management, and a persistent order summary. Checkout is reduced to three steps: contact, delivery, and payment. Each step keeps a short recap of the previous choices so people do not have to hold the whole order in their head.

What I learned

Constraints make commerce calmer. Limiting the palette and the type scale made it easier to give product photography a consistent stage. Reusing content blocks also made the interface feel more like a publication and less like an endless grid of tiles.

The next step for a production build would be real product data, a commerce backend, and usability testing with people who shop for homeware regularly. The prototype is intentionally scoped as a design and front-end concept, not a live store.
